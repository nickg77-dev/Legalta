# Deck copy rules

Read this file whenever the output is slide copy: pptx content, a deck outline, or text
that will sit inside a card, box, or chart. Adapted from Nick's Ethos grading calibration,
rewritten for authoring. Deck mode also has personality OFF (see SKILL.md) - the register
here is plain and functional throughout, not conversational.

Different elements on a slide hold to different bars. Collapsing them into one standard is
the single biggest source of weak deck copy - both writing everything too loosely (titles
that don't say what the slide is about) and writing everything too stiffly (turning a chart
legend into a full clause).

---

## Main slide title

Not exempt from anything. Not rescued by being accurate, and not rescued by content
elsewhere on the slide - a title that doesn't describe its own slide is a weak title even
if a bullet three lines down makes the real point clear. Test: could this just say what
the slide is about?

"Biofuels are a family of fuels - and the pathway determines the use case" -> "Biofuels in
Shipping." The elaborated version performs; it doesn't inform.

## Subtitle

Checked against whether it actually relates to the rest of the slide, not just whether
it's grammatically clean. A subtitle introducing a topic nothing else on the slide develops
is dead weight, however well-formed the sentence is.

## Kicker / eyebrow (small label above or beside the title)

Held to a duplication test. If it restates content the title already states - not just
shares a topic, but says the same thing - cut it. A kicker earns its place by adding
something the title doesn't, not by echoing it in fewer words.

## Card heading

Plain noun-phrase headings naming a topic are fine: "Manufacturing & exports," "Cashless
payments," "Domestic travel recovery." Headings built on an abstract-motion noun that never
says what's moving are weak: "Digital media expansion" - expansion of what? Name the thing.

## Card body sentence - the strictest bar on the slide, sentence budget ENFORCED

Default to rewriting nearly any full sentence with more than one clause. Specifically
check for and fix without asking:

- An unsupported superlative or intensifier ("outstanding," "best-in-class,"
  "significantly," "strategically," "at the forefront of"). Cut it or replace with the
  specific fact it's standing in for.
- A trailing qualifier clause tacked onto an otherwise complete claim that hedges vaguely
  or introduces an unrelated point. Either fold it into a real second sentence or cut it.
- A broken or run-on grammatical join ("has focused... and while progressively
  gaining..."). Rebuild the sentence.
- More than roughly fifteen to twenty words, or more than two or three enumerated items.
  Split it or cut items.
- Two or more stacked hyphenated modifiers in a row.
- A short declarative fragment (two to four words, full stop) used as a headline-style
  opener immediately followed by an elaborating sentence in the same bullet ("Broad
  pyrogen coverage. For a CHO-derived monoclonal antibody..."). Merge into one plain
  sentence or split into a genuine label-plus-body pair.

This is enforced, not advisory: rewrite over-budget card body sentences as part of the
pass rather than flagging them and asking. If Nick has deliberately written a long line
for a specific reason, he'll say so and it stays; otherwise, tighten it.

A sentence only survives untouched when it states one clean, specific fact in one clause
with no added intensifier, no tacked-on clause, and no unglossed jargon: "Lower energy
density means larger tanks and more frequent bunkering" is fine as-is - one causal claim,
plainly worded, nothing appended.

## Box / stat-card label

A short label naming what its own box is about ("SERVICE," "VOLUME," "EXECUTION") is fine
at any length down to one word - brevity alone isn't a problem here. But check each word
inside a multi-word label for whether it's doing distinguishing work. "DROP-IN DIESEL
BIOFUELS" - "drop-in" adds nothing once the comparison is already between named
alternatives; cut to "Diesel Biofuels."

Standalone KPI / stat-card labels (a large pulled-out figure with a short identifying
label) are fine even when they restate a figure given elsewhere on the slide - a number
meant to be legible on its own isn't redundant just because the same number appears in a
bullet too.

## Chart legend label

Fine abbreviated relative to its card heading. "Exports" as a legend label is fine even
where the card heading reads "Manufacturing & exports" - a legend only needs to identify
its segment.

## Chart title and chart-embedded centre text

Judged as prose, same as any other phrase - not treated as structural furniture. A donut
or pie centre label paired with a decorative tagline that just restates the chart title is
weak; a bare figure with no tagline is closer to a stat-card label and is fine.

## Banner label with no connection to its content

Any label asserting positive framing with no link to what's beneath it - "A NEW ERA," "
MOMENTUM AHEAD," "UNLOCKING POTENTIAL" - gets deleted, not softened.

## Running footer (deck or section name, date, page number)

Fine when it names what the document is - a title-like footer identifying the deck. Not
fine when it asserts something about the document the brief never asked for: "CONFIDENTIAL
AND PROPRIETARY," "DRAFT," "INTERNAL USE ONLY." Test: does the footer name the document, or
claim something about it? The former stays; the latter goes unless specifically
instructed.

Footers never use a vertical bar, em dash, or semicolon as a separator - use a hyphen with
spaces, or split into two lines.

## Source line

Two checks: does it name an actual identifiable source (a document, organisation, or
dataset - not just "industry guidance"), and does everything after the source name add
information the slide doesn't already make obvious. If either fails, tighten or cut.

## Short callout / section label (one to three words)

Survives as short and plain when it traces to something the brief actually asked for, or
accurately names its section's function. A label introducing content the brief never
mentioned - a department name, an internal team name - doesn't belong just because it's
short.

Also check the label isn't editorialising beyond what its content supports: a callout
labelled "Regulatory intent" over content that's actually about a methodology choice is
mischaracterising, not just brief.

## Unfamiliar terms are not automatically a problem

Standard domain terminology used correctly is fine on a slide even to a generalist reader.
Don't simplify a term just because it's technical - simplify it only when a plainer word
carries the exact same meaning, or when the term itself has no clear referent. See
five-lens.md's density and jargon sections; the same test applies here.
