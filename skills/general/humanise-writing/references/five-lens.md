# The five-lens check

Adapted from the grading rubric Nick uses on Feather (ai-slop-writing-check /
ai-slop-professional-writing), rewritten here for authoring rather than scoring. No
verdicts, no scores, no ranking - just the underlying test and how to fix what it catches.

The governing question for every sentence, label, or heading: could this be rewritten
significantly better? Run this after the mechanical strip (banned words, cliché patterns,
hard rules), as the second, judgement-based pass.

---

## 1. Awkward or unnatural wording

Wording that doesn't sit naturally - strained, hard to parse, a noun forced to act as an
adjective. A human would have phrased it more plainly.

- "Contributions waned over the period" -> "Contributions decreased over the period."
- "A closer 2021 step-up from a small base" -> "A larger increase in 2021 from a low
  starting point." (name both sides of the comparison)
- "Shopping has become more deliberate and value-led" -> "more deliberate and focused on
  value."

Fix: say the thing plainly, in words a person would actually use.

## 2. Excessive or flowery wording

Reaches for a grander register than the point needs - inflated adjectives, buzzword
stacks, phrases that sound polished and action-oriented while saying very little.

- "Stronger AI performance for next-generation flagship smartphones" -> drop "flagship,"
  it's already implied.
- "Translate priorities into coordinated execution" -> "Translate priorities into a clear
  plan."
- "The organisation seeks to holistically cultivate a multifaceted ecosystem of
  synergistic capabilities" -> "The organisation wants to build a set of capabilities that
  work together."

Fix: strip the elevation, state the message in ordinary words.

## 3. Vague or empty wording

The reader can't tell what's actually being claimed - which product, which outcome, what
caused what - because the wording stays abstract where something specific was needed.

- "Broad-based usage across products" -> never says which products. Name them, or say
  "usage increasing across products" if that's genuinely all you know.
- "Progressive promotion improves trust and usability" -> "Customer rewards programmes
  improve trust and usability."
- Unanchored nouns: volume, quality, capacity, efficiency, throughput, performance,
  leverage, network, demand. Fine as a short label ("Volume" as a box heading). Vague the
  moment they appear in a full sentence making a claim without saying what they refer to.
  "Expected decline in volume" - volume of what? Sales, shipments, bookings? Say which.

Fix: name the specific product, metric, driver, or relationship the abstraction was
standing in for.

## 4. Unnecessary wording

Deleting the word or phrase costs the sentence nothing, or actively clarifies it.

- "Practical consumer needs" - "consumer needs" already implies practical. Drop "practical."
- "Operating earnings after defined non-GAAP adjustments" - "defined" clarifies nothing.
- "One verbatim follow-up to reveal what matters most" - the tail adds nothing; cut it.

Fix: delete it. If the sentence reads the same without the word, it was never carrying
anything.

## 5. Illogical wording

The words don't hold together: a verb that can't take its object, a linkage that produces
nonsense, a figure whose relationship to its comparison can't be worked out.

- "Test, learn, and scale the most promising ideas" - you can't learn an idea -> "Develop,
  test, and scale the most promising ideas."
- "79% Transit use back to pre-pandemic" - ambiguous - "Transit use has recovered to 79%
  of its pre-pandemic level."

Fix: repair the linkage so the sentence can only be read one way, and say which comparison
a figure is measured against.

---

## Comparatives: a narrower test than it looks

Not every unquantified comparative is Vague/empty. "Lower energy density means larger
tanks and more frequent bunkering" is fine despite "lower," "larger," and "more frequent"
all being unquantified - because these describe one self-contained causal chain, and the
relationship is domain-natural without needing a number.

Contrast with a comparative implying some other unstated thing being compared against:
"grew faster" (than what?), "industry-leading" (leading whom, by what measure?). The
missing side of the comparison is exactly the information the reader needs.

Test: does the sentence name both sides of what's being compared (even implicitly, as one
causal chain), or does it gesture at an external comparator it never states? The former is
fine unquantified. The latter needs the comparator named or the claim cut.

---

## Density is its own axis, independent of accuracy

A sentence can be completely accurate, well-targeted at its audience, and still fail
because it's doing too much in one breath. Judge length and clause-count separately from
whether the content is right.

- Five or more items in one clause: too dense regardless of how distinct each item is.
- Several stacked qualifying clauses ("as applicable," "where available," multiple
  prepositional phrases in sequence): break it up.
- Two or more hyphenated compound modifiers in a row ("human-cell, broad-spectrum
  in-vitro"): simplify or restructure as a plain sentence, even if each compound is a
  legitimate term on its own.

Default: one clause, one idea, no more than two or three enumerated items, no stacked
hyphenated modifiers. This is the sentence budget - see deck-copy.md for how it's enforced
in slide bodies specifically; it applies just as much to a paragraph in a covering letter
or a proposal.

Unfamiliar or technical terms are not, by themselves, a problem. Standard terminology used
correctly is fine even to a generalist reader. The jargon question is really one of the
five lenses above in disguise - is there a plainer word that means the same thing
(Excessive), does the term have no clear referent (Vague), or does it not actually cohere
with the rest of the sentence (Illogical)?
